Pre-registered study · Think-Reason-Learn · VerifiableRL

Can PPO or GRPO train VerifiableRL better than the optimiser Think-Reason-Learn ships?

PPO can: it clearly beats TRL's rollout-based optimiser at equal compute, in about half the time. GRPO as published cannot. A repaired GRPO does at a short budget and does not hold up over a long one.

22 November 2026 TRL v0.4.4 @ a712645 6 datasets × 2 reward regimes × 10 seeds 600 confirmatory runs

The short version

  • 1PPO clearly beats TRL's shipped optimiser at equal compute: +0.50 at the standard budget (9 of 12 conditions won, none lost) and +0.55 at four times it, in about half the wall-clock time.
  • 2TRL's optimiser cannot learn when to stop. Its STOP target never sees slot costs. In the cost-aware regime it reveals exactly its minimum number of slots on every dataset. As shipped, it does not improve with four times the budget.
  • 3GRPO as published does not beat TRL's optimiser. It is level with it overall, worse where stopping matters, and 0.50 behind PPO (95% CI 0.41 to 0.59).
  • 4One change repairs GRPO for a short run: do not divide by the group's standard deviation. That division makes a sample whose episodes differ only by a slot cost count as much as one whose episodes differ by a right or wrong call. Without it ("mean-centred GRPO") the method beats TRL's optimiser by 0.41 at the standard budget and is within 0.09 of PPO. Given four times the budget, it collapses late in training in 8 of 12 runs.
  • 5Of the two challengers, PPO is the robust one: best at both budgets, fewest failed runs.

Normalised return: 0 is a random policy, 1 is the best policy that does not adapt to what it sees. Above 1, the policy gains by adapting.

Where the question came from

The study started from a narrower question: TRL was believed to train its reinforcement-learning model with PPO, and the question was whether GRPO would do better. The first step was an audit of the code. TRL has one reinforcement-learning model, VerifiableRL: a policy network chooses which slot of information about a sample to reveal next (education, jobs, and so on) or to stop, and a classifier predicts from what was revealed.

Its policy is not trained by PPO, or by any policy gradient. For each state an episode visits, every slot is revealed in turn and the current policy is rolled out ten times; the policy is then fitted to a softmax of those rollout values, and STOP is fitted to a sigmoid of the reward for stopping right now. "PPO" appears nowhere in the code or its history.

That made both methods challengers to the optimiser TRL actually ships, which is the comparison this page is organised around. The hypotheses had been registered around GRPO and are reported as registered.

  • Only the optimiser varies. The networks, the action rule, the rewards and the deployment loop are imported from TRL and identical for every arm. Tests check this state by state.
  • Equal compute. Every run gets the compute of one default TRL pass over the training set, counted on every network, PPO's critic included.
  • Equal tuning. 24 random configurations per learner on separate development seeds; one frozen configuration each for all datasets.
  • Two reward regimes. R1 is TRL's defaults exactly. R2 is a cost-aware regime built from TRL's own arguments, in which each slot has a price and stopping may come after one slot.
  • Six datasets. A synthetic problem whose best strategy is known, 3,000 generated founder profiles, Kickstarter projects, job postings, credit default, census income.
  • Pre-registered. Hypotheses, metrics, statistics and the decision rule were committed before any experiment. Development seeds and confirmatory seeds never mix.

At this budget, PPO against mean-centred GRPO is close. The estimate is 0.09 in PPO's favour, with an interval that excludes zero before the multiplicity correction and not after. Ten seeds resolve differences of about 0.17. The longer run below is what separates them.

Why canonical GRPO fails here

GRPO scores each episode against the other episodes of the same sample and divides by their standard deviation. In language-model training, rewards are usually right-or-wrong, so a group that agrees has no spread and contributes nothing. Here every episode also carries small, real cost differences. In 39% of groups all episodes end in the same call, so their returns differ by a few hundredths, and dividing by that spread turns a step penalty of 0.1 into an advantage as large as the gap between a true and a false positive.

Measured at the initial policy, with no training: canonical GRPO's update has a cosine of 0.24 with the true policy gradient, averaged over conditions, and −0.86 on one of them. Without the division it is 0.99. The division gives the shortest episodes in a group a strong positive advantage they have not earned, so the policy learns to stop as early as it is allowed.

Same episodes, same clipped objective, same settings; only the baseline changes. The two arms that divide by the group's standard deviation are the two that fail. Among the rest, the choice of baseline matters much less than expected.

The per-sample baseline was expected to beat any learned critic, because a group of episodes on one sample shares the hidden label and a critic cannot see it. The order came out as predicted (per-sample mean 1.25, a critic given the label 1.19, an ordinary critic 1.13) but none of those gaps is established. With episodes of one to five steps, variance is not what limits learning.

Does it hold up?

Through the real fit, with the classifier co-trained

The main experiment froze the classifier so that only the optimiser varied. TRL does not: it co-trains the classifier on the states the policy reaches. So the proposed patch was run through the public API on two datasets and both regimes, five seeds each. The shipped optimiser was given three passes; the group optimiser, one.

Group size

Mean-centred GRPO scores between 1.11 and 1.25 for every group size from 2 to 32: two episodes per sample already give a usable baseline. Canonical GRPO is poor at every size and worst at two, where a z-score can only be +1 or −1.

TRL's default optimiser is not under-trained: four times the budget changes nothing. PPO keeps improving. Mean-centred GRPO does not hold its gains: part-way through it is level with PPO, and then it drops sharply, usually between two checkpoints, with no sign of over-fitting.

A diagnosis was registered before the rest of that experiment was read: the batch-wide scale the advantages are divided by shrinks as the policy converges, so the step never does. A one-line change to a scale that does not shrink cost nothing at the standard budget and helped a little at four times it (0.95 to 1.12), but 6 of 12 runs still collapsed. The diagnosis is at most part of the story.

What this means for TRL

Against the shipped optimiser, PPO's lead is not close. Two properties of the shipped optimiser explain most of the gap: its STOP target is a function of the immediate call only, so costs never reach the stopping decision; and it spends its budget labelling each visited state with sixty rollouts, where a whole-episode method learns from every decision of every episode it plays.

Recommendation. Offer a whole-episode policy-gradient optimiser in VerifiableRL, and make it PPO: best at both budgets, fewest failed runs. Canonical GRPO should not be offered. The pre-registered rule, taken to the letter, selects mean-centred GRPO, because it passed both of its tests at the standard budget; the longer run shows it is not yet safe to train for long, so the rule's answer is reported and not followed.

A TRL-native implementation of the critic-free variant is written and tested, as a concrete starting point: VerifiableRL(optimizer="group"), default behaviour unchanged. Under TRL's own lock file it passes ruff, pyright in strict mode with no errors, and the full offline test suite (494 tests). Its documentation states the long-run limitation. A TRL-native PPO was not written.

from think_reason_learn.verifiable_rl import VerifiableRL

A side finding about the default reward. With TP +4, FP −16 and a call threshold of 0.3, a positive call pays on average only when it is right about 80% of the time. On every real dataset here the classifier is right far less often, so the return-maximising policy is one that leads the classifier to call positive as rarely as possible, and a better optimiser finds it more reliably. On the founder data PPO has the highest return and the lowest F0.5. That is a property of the reward, worth knowing when setting reward_fp and reward_threshold.

What this study cannot say

  • Small differences. The planned power would have needed more than 100 seeds; ten were run. The longer-budget, end-to-end and follow-up experiments have three to five seeds on four conditions.
  • Why mean-centred GRPO collapses over a long run. One diagnosed cause accounts for part of it; no remedy was validated.
  • Anything about real founders. The founder profiles are generated, and VCBench was not available.
  • Behaviour at larger scale. 2,000 training samples, six or seven slots, small networks on a CPU.
  • What the paper says. The VerifiableRL paper could not be retrieved; the incumbent is the library code.
  • PPO and GRPO were written for this study. They are tested against hand-computed advantages, TRL's own returns and a problem with a known optimum, but they are one implementation each.
